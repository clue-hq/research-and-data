<p>Ryan Embree:<br />
Welcome to Suite Spot, where hoteliers check in and we check out what&#8217;s trending in hotel marketing. I&#8217;m your host, Ryan Embree. Hello everyone, and welcome to another episode of The Suite Spot. This is your host, Ryan Embree here with a very exciting, informative episode continuing our TMG Hospitality Trailblazers. This is our series where we are talking to those industry leaders and brands, management companies that are paving the way forward. I&#8217;ve got an absolutely amazing guest, I&#8217;m excited to speak with him today. Ben Rafter, CEO of Hotel Equities. Ben, thank you so much for joining the Suite Spot.</p>
<p>Ben Rafter:<br />
Thanks, Ryan. Looking forward to it.</p>
<p>Ryan Embree:<br />
Yeah, we&#8217;re gonna have fun conversation, talk all about hotel equities, but on the Suite Spot we do a little tradition where instead of just doing your normal bio, I actually like handing it over to our guests to kind of talk through their hospitality journey. because sometimes you get a little bit more insights. Us hospitality people, we got mentors. We come from different brands, sometimes fall into hospitality, quite frankly. You know, talk to us, our Suite Spot hotel audience, a little bit about your hospitality journey and what led you to your role as a CEO over at Hotel Equities.</p>
<p>Ben Rafter:<br />
Sure, I fit into the fall into it category, without a doubt. I was a, uh, tech guy for better part of 15 years, mostly startups. Sold two of &#8217;em and after selling, the second one was locked out of the industry for a year and got a phone call from a hotel guy. And he said, what are you doing? We packed up and headed over to Nepal and on about the third day he said to me, so what do you think of the hospitality industry and hotels? And it was dumb enough to open my mouth and give him my opinion that you needed to sort of fuse together larger than life hoteliers, which was his background and kind of quant tech data, guys like me. And over the next 20 days, we hashed out starting a hotel company literally on the side of a mountain, in this case. And after that ended, it was 2008, the market crashed. We had a new president. I flew to Hawaii and we started with four hotels in Hawaii. And it&#8217;s been nonstop ever since. So definitely not a traditional way to get into the industry.</p>
<p>Ryan Embree:<br />
Yeah, yeah. You give new meaning to kind of climbing the corporate ladder, so to speak, when, when talking about that. But I think that&#8217;s what honestly makes hospitality so fascinating. Because yours, honestly, a lot of the majority of the guests we talked to Ben, hospitality was not their final destination, so to speak, what you were talking to. But I think it brings new perspective and obviously insights into an industry that, quite frankly, we&#8217;re gonna talk about it in a minute, but needs some more technology integration into hospitality right now. And to fast forward to today, obviously a strategic merger in May, 2025 with Springboard Hospitality. It&#8217;s been extremely busy second half of the year for you and your team, including a couple exciting announcements we&#8217;re gonna talk about in a second, Ben. But, you know, can you share some of the biggest kind of takeaways and lessons you&#8217;ve learned for the business, over this past six months? Maybe give us a state of the Hotel Equities as you would say.</p>
<p>Ben Rafter:<br />
Yeah, absolutely. I mean one thing, coming from sort of independent and generally either gateway market or leisure focused. You learn how diverse some of these markets are and how much different it is running a courtyard or a Rest Inn or a Hampton in a suburb versus running a resort in the middle of Waikiki. And it&#8217;s been great kind of seeing both sides of the spectrum there. And then trying to figure out how initiatives or which initiatives work across the entire entity. And then which of &#8217;em should be limited to $400 a night hotel or a select service hotel. And I gotta say, coming from the independent space where we&#8217;re all about content and drone shots and Instagram and social media and things like that, obviously walking into a suburban flagged select service hotel is a whole different, a whole different ball of wax, so to speak. So that part&#8217;s been great. We have great leaders in all of those divisions and it&#8217;s been great working with them.</p>
<p>Ryan Embree:<br />
Yeah, I mean, just adds to the exposure, again, what you were talking about. And to add to that, right. You know, in October you were back on a panel discussing outdoor centric hotels at the lodging conference in Phoenix, we talk about the spectrum of from lifestyle, and now you&#8217;ve got this experiential glamping. This was the perfect topic for you and hotel equities, obviously through your management of postcard cabins, which is with Marriott&#8217;s new outdoor collection. Talk to us, how about how you guys are really capitalizing on this trend, experiential travel, that glamping that now even the big brands are trying to get into?</p>
<p>Ben Rafter:<br />
Yeah, every, everybody&#8217;s trying to get into it. So we were on all of the postcard cabins, and that was kind of the feature point of Marriott&#8217;s launch of the outdoor, their outdoor segment. It&#8217;s one, it&#8217;s been great. It&#8217;s an area that&#8217;s growing two and a half, a little more than two and a half times faster than the regular hotel on the street. Now, of course, you break that down by segments it maybe two times and maybe three times. And it&#8217;s also so new for the brands that they&#8217;re trying to sort of get their arms around it. And because you, you just have to think when you&#8217;re staying in an outdoor experience, you&#8217;re not staying in a city. And when you search on Marriott or Hilton or Hyatt or whatever, the first thing you usually do is you type in New York City, you type in Seattle, you type in whatever. Well, if you&#8217;re gonna stay in the middle of the national forest where the nearest city is 2000 people and you don&#8217;t know what it&#8217;s called, like how do you even search for this kind of stuff? And then you combine that with staffing, where, the GM better have a pickup truck and be able to go to the nearest Piggly Wiggly or whatever to go find whatever&#8217;s needed because you may be an hour away from the nearest area to get the entity. There&#8217;s no Sysco truck driving by in the middle of the afternoon. And then high seasonality. And the big question I think for the industry is going to be how do you fill some of these things on a Wednesday afternoon in off season? Obviously we&#8217;re targeting groups in corporate and to make them kind of intimate experiences where 8, 10, 15, 20 people can get together. But it&#8217;s, it&#8217;s a totally different space. But the great thing is consumers are really interested in it because they&#8217;re interested in branching out from their traditional leisure stay, and now they can find this kind of stuff.</p>
<p>Ryan Embree:<br />
I mean, listen, as someone who climbed Everest, right, I mean that is all about the experience. The destination and getting to that is part of the journey and part of the experience, which I think is why people love it. And they&#8217;re gonna do a lot of your marketing for you to be honest with you, between the storytelling that they&#8217;re doing on places like social media, now all of a sudden they&#8217;re telling your story and, and people are, are, are just really seeing this in a time where we&#8217;re gonna talk about in a minute, technology&#8217;s kind of taking over, but being like, Hey, maybe this is a nice little escape for me and, and something different that is going to be an experience. You know, we talk about that all the time. And a true experience.</p>
<p>Ben Rafter:<br />
Yeah. And it doesn&#8217;t have to be outdoor travel. The great thing about this industry in general, and the way it&#8217;s evolving and I&#8217;m looking forward to talking more about technology, is that people want something more and more customized and more and more localized. It could be a food vacation, it could be outdoor travel, it could be adventure travel, which to me is a different category. And the way that we&#8217;re now interacting and searching for these experiences getting much more advanced and people can now find them. Versus before it was, I know I wanna stay outside, I&#8217;m gonna research some natural national parks, and where do I go from there? Right. And now it&#8217;s a wide open space.</p>
<p>Ryan Embree:<br />
You wanna see how many segments our industry has gotten chopped up into go to any hospitality conference. And you&#8217;re talking about heritage, travel, food travel, like you&#8217;re talking about travel, that&#8217;s multi-generational travel, right? It used to just be business and leisure. Which one are you on now? There are so many different ways and priorities the way that people and travelers are looking at travel through a completely different lens than they did a decade ago. And I think a lot of that, to be honest with you happened. What happened in 2020. It&#8217;s interesting to see brands, management companies adapt to those and try to be at the forefront of that because it&#8217;s these emerging markets and trends are popping up and they&#8217;re becoming widely popular.</p>
<p>Ben Rafter:<br />
Yeah, it got it, it was accelerated for sure. In 2020, I, there&#8217;s a comparison I like to make. Back, back when I was a kid and was learning how to ski, we had the little skinny narrow, super long skis. It was difficult. Snowboards came around and everyone complained about snowboards &#8217;cause they carved up the moguls or whatever. I can&#8217;t remember what it was, but ultimately the snowboards made skiing better and the ski technology advanced to what we have today. And I look at the emergence of Airbnb or things like that, they&#8217;ve really pushed the traditional sort of hotel lodging space to up its game. And I think we&#8217;re sort of seeing that with all these different concepts coming out. And I think it&#8217;s needed too. And we have one advantage over some of these, some of these transient vacation plays. And that is, at the end of the day, we still the human in most of the hotels. And I think we need to up the game in terms of the level of service that the people at the hotels provide and can provide to sort of make it so, Hey, I&#8217;m staying outdoors. I&#8217;m staying in a gateway city. I&#8217;m on a food vacation, whatever, but that person actually at the hotel is going to provide value to my vacation.</p>
<p>Ryan Embree:<br />
They&#8217;re gonna supplement it, sometimes even be a part of the experience there. I think that&#8217;s the big knock on what we see on Airbnb is, you have to be the housekeeper, you have to be the concierge, everything like that. And they&#8217;re starting to adjust, obviously with what they&#8217;re doing on the tech side. So it should be interesting to see, but you&#8217;re right that that competition I do think has accelerated that and really interested. We&#8217;re at a very cool inflection point right now. And speaking of, you talked about your strong tech background, Ben, you know, when you come into hospitality, where do you think we are right now in this an overall technology cycle? And then maybe you can drill down a little bit more into, when you look at your portfolio at Hotel Equities, what&#8217;s your approach to kind of this technology?</p>
<p>Ben Rafter:<br />
You may have heard the, or seen the big sigh on where we&#8217;re at in the industry. Since I got here has been behind, and there are a lot of reasons for it, but we&#8217;re gradually catching up. And AI although probably not over hyped, but over talked about AI will make a major difference in the hospitality industry because of the nature of what it is, much more so than other industries. We&#8217;re just not quite there yet. So, a long-winded way of saying we&#8217;re still behind. We have a lot of legacy backbone issues and technology in general. We have too many startups chasing too few solutions and then trying to prove that they can do other solutions. But the great tipping point coming up is how people are searching for their hotel stay, whether business or leisure is changing dramatically, how the back end of the hotel is operating is changing dramatically. And some of these solutions that are being built are now targeting these areas. So I&#8217;m not excited about the past, but I&#8217;m pretty excited about the future because everyone wants to talk about the front end and search, and that&#8217;s probably the more exciting thing. But just think about scheduling workforce in a 300% hotel and how effective and efficient that is becoming. And we need it because margins are being eroded. RevPAR is flat. Well, expenses are up 5%. And so we need to be able to squeeze out every ounce of efficiency in all of these hotels. And our owners are all demanding it.</p>
<p>Ryan Embree:<br />
Yeah, you&#8217;re absolutely, I mean, the conversations we&#8217;ve had at covering hospitality conferences with leaders is talking about both sides of the desk. And the opportunity at both sides, because I do think we get that shiny object of the, how can we improve the guest experience andeven the sci-fi that robot coming down with the towels to do all that stuff, but it&#8217;s really gonna be those small efficiencies that get us to a closer place to what we&#8217;ve been talking about forever in our industry, personalization and, and this seamless traveler journey, right?</p>
<p>Ben Rafter:<br />
Yeah. And we can figure out what, you know, we try to define a great hotel experience historically, or luxury historically, and what&#8217;s the thread count and how many fixtures are in the bathroom and all this other stuff, which is important. But now we&#8217;re nearing a point where we can understand, okay, what really means luxury or a great hotel experience to this individual guest? And it may not be five fixtures and a 600 thread count. It may mean something else, right. And the level of personalization we have is nearing where we can define that experience when the person arrives. But yeah, so that&#8217;s, it&#8217;s, it&#8217;s all really exciting. And one of the great things we&#8217;re pretty aggressive with technology as you might guess. And so we beta a lot of different products and I want to continue doing so because although, three outta four may not work to the satisfaction that we have, or four outta five, you do find the ones that do, and we can embrace and adopt those really, really rapidly.</p>
<p>Ryan Embree:<br />
Yeah. I talked to a leader at the hospitality show back in October who said the modern hoteliers tech stack today is absolutely, it&#8217;s the most vast it&#8217;s been, and it&#8217;s gonna be very interesting to see, because so many people are doing what you&#8217;re doing, Ben, a lot of beta testing, a lot of trying out new technologies to try to calibrate it right to that point, to where we can be closer to personalization. And to your point about luxury, right? I mean, we&#8217;re even to a place now where if you&#8217;ve checked out White Lotus, the ultra luxury is actually removing technology altogether, right? So, but I think that&#8217;s, to your point of my luxury, could look very different from your luxury. And I think that&#8217;s what the true place of AI and technology is gonna get us to where we can personalize that perfect hotel experience no matter where we&#8217;re at.</p>
<p>Ben Rafter:<br />
Yeah. And there&#8217;s a debate that you&#8217;ve had on this podcast and had with other people I&#8217;m sure is does AI help or hurt the most for flags for OTAs, for independence? Where is it gonna make the biggest impact? And I won&#8217;t say where I think it&#8217;s gonna hurt the most, but I do think it really, really helps sort of soft brands and independence, because soft brands and independence have the ability to tell a story, and they can augment that story through technology content sort of finally is king. We&#8217;ve been talking about content being king for 20 years, and now content finally will be king because people are searching and AI knows what I want or what you want almost as well as you do, right? And so just think of how you&#8217;ve changed using Google over the last 10 years, where now you&#8217;re generally asking it questions instead of typing in prompts, multiply that by 10, and suddenly we have AI that says, oh, I&#8217;m going to Honolulu. I usually stay outta Westin because that&#8217;s what I know, or a Marriott or whatever. But AI can say, oh, you&#8217;re gonna Honolulu here, stay at this place because it has a restaurant that you&#8217;re gonna love. They make a great cocktail, and they have this thing that you indicated you liked when you were in New York City. And so this is your thing. And of course, what AI really gets advanced is gonna say, and I&#8217;ve booked it for you and I requested this, and I&#8217;m gonna monitor it for the next 30 days to make sure the revenue managers will screw up and lower the price. And if it does, I&#8217;ll cancel and rebook it for you.</p>
<p>Ryan Embree:<br />
Yeah. I mean, all of a sudden you&#8217;re telling your story to a little bit different audience with that and trying to capture that agent&#8217;s attention, maybe that, so that they can kind of put you front and center in the eyes of the traveler and the guests. But wanna switch our converse along the same lines, but more of a cautionary tale in hospitality and technology. What&#8217;s obviously headlining the news recently, the Marriott Sonder story, and you commented on this on LinkedIn. I saw Ben, you know, you said, and I quote Saunders shut down highlights just how complex our industry has become, where tech integration, long-term lease structures and multi-state partnership must align to create a sustainable operating model. So in a time when technology, right now, a lot of people would argue is, I mean, every day is at its peak, right? We&#8217;re peaking every single day in technology. Why is still having the right partnerships in alignment still so important? And, and we still see a Marriott Sonder story happen today?</p>
<p>Ben Rafter:<br />
Taking a step away from technology, I think the real lesson learned from Sonder, and it&#8217;s unfortunate because we learned it with WeWork too, is if you&#8217;re going to lease a bunch of properties, you better make sure, you can fill those properties through thick or thin because that lease payments come in due no matter what, right? Suddenly you&#8217;re representing yourself basically, and you&#8217;re trying to fill the owner&#8217;s shoes. And WeWork failed, and Sonder I think ultimately failed because of that. But underneath it, you have to make Sonder never carved itself out as, Hey, it&#8217;s seamless technology that gets me into my room with no interaction. And what is the additional value of that, and what will somebody pay for it? And then I think they plug into Marriott, I don&#8217;t know, obviously, but they plug into Marriott for broader reach and distribution. But now you&#8217;re searching New York City, you&#8217;ve got 30 other Marriotts who all have a well-defined brand, or probably a hundred other Marriotts in New York, you&#8217;re searching somewhere, right? And the seamless technology story just got lost in the whole thing. And then it&#8217;s like, okay, why am I gonna stay at this place over these other 20 choices I see on Mary&#8217;s site? And then ultimately the whole thing collapses. But it&#8217;s a classic case, a very long-winded way of saying it&#8217;s a classic case of technology for technology state sometime just doesn&#8217;t work.</p>
<p>Ryan Embree:<br />
