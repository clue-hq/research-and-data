# Comparison and alternatives

Make a consequential choice easier to understand. Compare what matters for a
particular task, not which brand deserves a column of green checks. Use
workflow-contrast for a change within one workflow; use objection-handling for
one concern about adopting the product. A generic “better than the rest” statement
is not a comparison.

## Establish a fair basis

Identify the alternatives, audience use case and one to three relevant criteria.
For named products, verify the current version, plan, region and date where these
affect the claim. Read each product's own relevant documentation; the advertiser's
comparison page is evidence of its positioning, not independent proof of every
competitor limitation. Retain the comparison basis beside any source IDs.

Use equivalent units and conditions: a monthly commitment price and an annual
prepaid price are different offers; a configurable capability is not necessarily
absent because it is not enabled by default. A check mark, omission, crossed-out
icon or unlabelled chart bar makes a factual claim just as words do. Verify each
meaningful visual encoding. Where evidence disagrees, mark the criterion unresolved
instead of awarding a winner.

Write copy around the buyer's decision: the task or constraint that makes one
approach relevant. State narrow differences plainly and preserve qualifications.
Use a comparison-guide or product-exploration action when that is the destination;
an artwork CTA is optional. Do not turn a capability difference into unsupported
savings, quality rankings or a universal recommendation.

Inspect the company's comparison ads and linked comparison content. Observe
whether they use direct naming, a category distinction, a use-case story or a
demonstration. Study the reading order, density, logo treatment and action.
Derive a visual relationship from those references, not a compulsory versus table.
Missing comparable evidence calls for a one-sided supported product statement,
clearly ending the comparison rather than disguising the rival as “legacy.”

## Three useful comparison forms

- **One criterion, visible difference:** native labels frame two authentic details
  or precisely supported values on the same basis. Useful when one decision
  matters enough to lead. Give qualifiers equal legibility on both sides.
- **Fit for the task:** native category or approach labels show which conditions
  suit each option. Useful when there are meaningful tradeoffs rather than one
  winner. Do not invent axes or scores to make a decorative quadrant.
- **The decisive demonstration:** one dominant authentic product view explains
  the differentiating capability, while a concise native statement names the
  alternative and the verified distinction. Useful when a table would duplicate
  what the picture already establishes.

Select the form from the evidence; hierarchy may favor the message while the
comparison remains fair. Competitor ridicule, illegible caveats and fake zeroes
weaken credibility even when the headline sounds plausible.

## Build and audit

Use `knowledge/scene/components.md` for native labels, rules, charts, qualifiers
and optional buttons. Place authentic advertiser and authorized comparison logos
as independent assets; logo size does not establish feature superiority. Use
`/.deepagent/skills/generative-imagery/SKILL.md` for independent background imagery. Generate
illustrative context only; never generate comparison evidence, competitor UI or
certification badges.

Follow `knowledge/scene/placement.md` for square and portrait. Reduce the number
of criteria or change the layout before reducing qualifiers to microtype. Review
every row, icon and implication against its source. If removing a footnote changes
who wins, the condition belongs in the main comparison. Report missing evidence
and choose a narrower supported statement when a fair comparison cannot be made.
