# Editorial point of view

Use this family when the audience should reconsider a familiar assumption or
recognize what the brand believes. The argument should have a useful consequence
for the audience. A general product slogan fits type-led-positioning; a practical
lesson fits educational-guide. Provocation alone is not a point of view, and
this family does not authorize fabricated market evidence.

## Locate the actual argument

Read the supplied essay, founder statement, research interpretation or approved
brand position. Identify its thesis, reasoning and audience consequence. Separate
opinion, observation, prediction and empirical fact in the copy. A belief can be
framed as a belief without invented statistics; a factual industry assertion
still needs an actual source with its date, population and scope. Do not turn
a forecast into a settled result or a personal opinion into company policy.

Use the brand's original editorial ads to judge cadence, confidence, humor and
how much explanation the audience expects. Sharpen the argument through specific
language rather than categorical exaggeration. A named author's words remain
their words: preserve exact quotations and attribution, or write an unquoted
brand summary that accurately reflects the source. Do not invent a founder
signature to make an ordinary line feel authoritative.

## Find a form that advances the thought

- **A typographic thesis:** Native words and purposeful emphasis make a specific
  position unmistakable, with only the reasoning necessary to make it credible.
  Useful when the language itself is distinctive; no mandatory supporting card.
- **An editorial image and argument:** Independent photography or conceptual
  artwork creates tension or context, and native copy resolves its relevance.
  Useful when the image contributes a thought rather than simply illustrating
  the headline's nouns.
- **An explained distinction:** Native labels and modest geometry clarify two
  ideas, a continuum or an argument's structure. Useful when the distinction is
  the insight; unequal forms must not imply measured differences without data.

Choose from the source's reasoning and inspected references, not a universal
manifesto layout. For a close companion, preserve the original ad's hierarchy,
typographic character, image treatment, logo relationship and CTA treatment.
Vary the actual argument or visual relationship intentionally. Do not remove
observed texture or expressive imagery to default to a flat statement panel.

## Compose a legible, attributable position

Read `knowledge/scene/components.md` and `knowledge/scene/references.md`.
Keep thesis, supporting reasoning, attribution and any CTA label native. Use
native geometry for emphasis, meaningful diagrams and buttons; keep authentic
logos, portraits and artwork independent. A portrait requires a real source and
a legitimate connection to the statement. Graphs require supported data and
labels; a visual argument is not permission to invent category adoption curves.

Let the thesis lead and the brand identify its publisher. An image may instead
earn the first read if the copy resolves it immediately. Artwork CTA presence
and treatment follow relevant original ads and the request; a sourced essay
destination supports an invitation to read, while a self-contained statement
may need no button. Use `knowledge/scene/placement.md` for square and portrait
adaptation, protecting the argument's line breaks and qualification rather than
cropping it down to a misleading slogan.

If no approved thesis can be recovered, derive only a supportable product position
when it meets the request, and record the missing editorial source. Counterexamples:
“everyone has abandoned” without evidence, an invented founder quote, or a clever
headline with no relation to the product audience. Probe “Make an ad about our
view of the industry” with an essay and without one; preserve the distinction
between the brand's opinion and claims about the wider world.
