# Customer spotlight

Make a particular customer's real work worth noticing. Use this family when
the customer identity, context and relationship are central. A single approved
quote or statistic can reuse attributed-proof; a multi-stage transformation may
need a case-study narrative. A logo wall is not a customer story, and an account
targeted by an ABM campaign must not be portrayed as an existing customer.

## Establish whose story this is

Read the approved story or official source. Confirm the relationship, named
organization, particular team or person, product used and supported activity or
result. Keep a team-level experience at that scope; do not make it a company-wide
deployment. Check what the supplied campaign context authorizes for customer
logos, portraits, quotes and co-branding. An available logo file is not evidence
of endorsement or consent to an invented joint announcement.

Choose the customer's meaningful task, experience or achievement as the angle.
A verified factual narrative can carry the creative without a quote or metric.
When quoting, preserve the approved words and attribution; do not manufacture
first-person language from a third-person case study. Use the advertiser's
editorial voice to frame the story, and preserve the customer's distinct voice
where it is actually sourced. Avoid reducing the customer to a proof badge.

## Choose a protagonist-led composition

- **Person and practice:** An authentic portrait or working-context photograph
  anchors a short, sourced account of the person's task. Useful when an actual
  participant gives the story specificity; no synthetic stand-in for that person.
- **Customer world:** The customer's real product, venue or brand imagery leads,
  with a concise statement of the supported relationship. Useful when what the
  customer makes is more recognizable than an employee portrait.
- **Editorial co-brand feature:** Native story title and context lead with two
  independent authentic identities and optional supporting imagery. Useful when
  no suitable portrait exists; it need not look like a press-release lockup.

Let inspected original customer campaigns establish the advertiser/customer
hierarchy and CTA treatment. Preserve supplied lockups, clear space and identity
colors; optical balance does not require equal logo boxes. If approved references
make the customer dominant, keep that role in a close companion. Vary the story
angle or authentic scene deliberately without inventing another relationship.

## Assemble without merging identities

Read `knowledge/scene/components.md` and `knowledge/scene/references.md`.
Headlines, attribution, story text and CTA labels stay native; frames and button
geometry stay native. Keep each logo, portrait, product image and artwork as an
independent layer. Do not generate a combined customer/advertiser logo or bake
native attribution into the photograph. A native graph needs actual scoped
results; no decorative growth curve should imply customer performance.

Select the artwork CTA from the relevant original ads and request, matching the
real destination, such as the full story. Use `knowledge/scene/placement.md` for
square/portrait recomposition. Preserve recognizable identities, readable scope
and a humane crop; a face and the attribution referring to it must still belong
together in the reading order.

Without a confirmed relationship, do not create the requested spotlight. Explain
the missing evidence and use an anonymous supported scenario only when the user
allows that alternate purpose. Missing metrics or portraits alone need not block
an evidence-backed editorial story. Counterexamples: a prospect labeled customer,
a quote synthesized from a blog, or vendor branding that overwhelms the featured
customer. Probe “Feature our customer in an ad” with a narrative-only source and
again with only a logo; the first can work, the second cannot establish the story.
