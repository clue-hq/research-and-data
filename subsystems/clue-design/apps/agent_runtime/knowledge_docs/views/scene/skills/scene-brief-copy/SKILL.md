# Brief and copy

Read the user request, brand card, `seller/index.md`, available evidence and asset
inventory in one gather; `seller/` holds the brand's own knowledge documents (company,
products, audience) and the account reads answer for a named target company (see
account-adaptation). Shortlist relevant advertiser examples, product imagery and supplied
references; view their pixels before choosing copy or treatment. Start with a
small useful set and broaden to resolve a named gap. Distinguish what the examples
actually show from metadata and assumptions. Derive the
audience, single useful message, medium and appropriate action from this context.
For “make an ad” with no size or placement in the person's message or the Head of
ABM's brief, ask as clarify-design-brief says, with a 1080 square awareness
creative as the default. `knowledge/scene/placement.md` governs adaptation and sets how
much copy each format carries: read the budget for your format before you write copy,
and write within it. Do not ask the user to become the copywriter.

When addressing a named target company or adapting approved creative for an
account, read [account-adaptation](/.deepagent/skills/account-adaptation/SKILL.md)
alongside the selected family. It governs identity, evidence limits, advertiser
preservation and real target-logo use; account-specific guidance is not a new
communicative family.

Infer brand voice from approved copy and inspected advertiser examples: directness,
vocabulary, rhythm, specificity, degree of playfulness and how the brand addresses
its audience. Describe the evidence in the direction; a font or color is not voice.
Use reference copy to infer voice and claim limits. For a new brief, write from
one evidenced advertiser capability and the supplied audience's task; existing
reference wording is not automatically the answer to that brief. Preserve exact
wording when requested. A shared campaign headline can remain when its support
or requested companion copy carries the relevant argument. Keep only the support
the message needs. Write lines a reader gets at a glance. Each rule below has a weaker
and a stronger line from made-up companies in different industries. They show the move,
not the words: don't reuse their wording, structure or numbers; find the same move in
this brand's own evidence and voice. A number goes into copy only when the evidence
gives it.

- **One idea per ad.** If a line needs a second idea to make sense, cut it or pick
  another angle.
- **Clear before clever.** The reader should first see what this is and that it's for
  them. The headline must make sense on its own, before anyone reads the support line;
  wit is welcome when it's still easy to get.
- **Concrete and checkable.** A real result, number, name or situation says more than
  an adjective.
  Weaker: "Dramatically faster month-end close." "Dramatically" can't be checked.
  Stronger (a finance tool whose customer case documents both numbers): "Month-end
  close in 4 days instead of 9."
- **Name the reader or their situation** when that is how they'll see it's for them.
- **Show the brand's proof rather than describing the brand:** a result, a customer or
  a quote you have evidence for.
  Weaker: "The most trusted name in freight." A claim anyone can make.
  Stronger (a logistics platform with a documented customer result): "Northline cut
  empty truck miles by a fifth." A named customer and a result, both from the evidence.

Use provided claims accurately;
quotes, numbers and customer attributions need actual evidence. Keep source IDs
with factual copy. Missing proof is a reason to narrow or qualify the message,
or choose nonfactual positioning. Labeling a product promise `positioning` does
not make it nonfactual. Compare the whole meaning, including implied timing,
control, causality, availability and authority, with the source; imperative grammar
alone does not remove a factual relationship. For quantitative proof, preserve the
metric and the subject, comparison and qualifiers needed to interpret it in the
visible composition, across layers if useful. Narrow or omit proof whose necessary
context is unavailable; never invent missing qualifiers. Review every actual occurrence
under `knowledge/scene/review.md`. For a CTA, decide whether this intent needs an artwork
action at all, whether the channel already supplies it, and how this advertiser's
relevant examples express it: absent, inline text, a link, a button or another
supported form. On LinkedIn and other feeds the platform already shows a button, so many ads
need no CTA in the artwork. When one is needed, write a specific verb and object for the
real next step, tied to what the viewer just read: what they'll get, not only where
they'll go. Avoid stock phrases. Do not default every
creative to a pill. Derive shape, typography and spacing from
observed brand examples and remeasure for the words. A proof or workflow story
need not end in a button. Keep channel headline/body/action distinct from artwork
copy; the record's `copy` contains only the independent artwork text layers.

Choose the angle before the playbook. First work out, from what you can read:
- Who is reading: the persona for this audience under `seller/audience/` (the index
  names it): their role, what they answer for, what worries them and what they want.
  For a named target company, also read its research (see account-adaptation).
- What proof there is: `seller/proof/` and the product documents, for results,
  customers and quotes you could use. Proof you can show is often the strongest
  message.
- What the ad is for: most feed ads reach people who aren't buying yet, so aim to be
  remembered, by linking the brand to a situation they'll recognise when they do
  need it. An ad for an offer, an event or a trial asks for one clear action.

Read the persona and the proof together in one batch, and wait for them. Then choose
the angle this reader, this proof and this purpose support best: a
result, a comparison with how they work today, an offer, something useful to learn,
an event, a customer's story, or a problem and its fix. Find its playbook through
the discovered skill descriptions or `knowledge/scene/catalog.md` and read it in your
next step. The playbook depends on what those reads tell you, so it never belongs in
the same batch. Choose problem-solution only when a recognisable problem is the strongest thing you
have. The catalog is derived from
the registered skill descriptions; no separate list of family names is maintained
here. Blend a relevant supporting technique when evidence and intent call for it;
record the main family in the direction record. These are procedures, not a template menu.
Load another relevant body or re-read one during
composition, review or revision. A selection is not a one-time discovery lock.
Already loaded shared guides need no second read through a wrapper skill.
If its evidence or assets are unavailable, pick a supportable direction and note
the limitation; ask only as clarify-design-brief says.
Preserve exact copy when the user supplies it; don't rewrite control
inputs to make the composition easier.

In creative-direction.json, record concise assumptions, voice evidence, visual
invariants and deliberate variations rather than asking routine aesthetic
questions.
