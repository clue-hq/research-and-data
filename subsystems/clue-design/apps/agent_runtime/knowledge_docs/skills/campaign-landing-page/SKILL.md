# Campaign Landing Page — build the argument, then the page

You are about to make or edit a page whose job is to move a visitor toward a
decision. Read this first, before any composing, pinning, or slot work.

## Why this skill exists

A landing page fails in one predictable way: it is built as a collection of
good-looking sections instead of as ONE ARGUMENT aimed at ONE DECISION. The
visitor arrived carrying context — an ad they clicked, an email they were sent,
a search they typed, a link a colleague forwarded. Every element that does not
continue that context is friction. Every link that starts a different journey
is a paid-for visitor walking away from the argument someone paid to present.

The measurable version of this is settled practice: pages whose links match
their goals (a 1:1 attention ratio) convert dramatically better than pages
full of exits; a single clear call to action beats five competing ones; a
headline that echoes what the visitor clicked beats a clever one; a dedicated
page per campaign beats one page absorbing six campaign angles; forms above
four fields bleed completions. You do not need to cite these numbers — you
need to build like they are true, because they are.

So this skill's one-sentence purpose:

> Construct the argument first — who is arriving, what they already believe,
> what they must come to believe, what single action closes the decision, and
> what evidence carries each step. Then build ONLY the sections that argument
> needs, and delete everything else.

Everything below is judgment for you to exercise. NOTHING here is a gate: no
rule in this file refuses work, blocks a build, or overrides what the user
asked for. When the user has stated a preference, their preference wins and
you follow it. When they have not, you decide — and you can explain why.

## Step 1 — Establish the brief

From the user's message, the design brief, and any research or material handed
to you, establish five things:

| Element | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| Traffic source | How is the visitor arriving, and what did they see before this page? |
| Audience | Who is the primary reader — role, situation, sophistication? |
| Offer | What does the visitor RECEIVE if they act? |
| Action | What single behavior counts as success? |
| Evidence | What proof, facts, numbers, names, and claims may the page use? |

**Material the user supplies is trusted as-is.** Research, priorities, numbers,
customer names, quotes — if it arrived in the brief or the conversation, it is
yours to use directly. You never verify it away, never water it down, never
replace it with a placeholder.

**When something is missing, assume and say so — don't stall.** State your
assumption in one plain line of your report ("no offer was named — I assumed a
demo request") and proceed. Ask the user in chat only when the missing piece
genuinely forks the page into two different arguments (a demo-request page and
a free-trial page are different arguments; ask which). One question, plainly
worded, then continue with the answer.

## Step 2 — Read the visitor's awareness stage

Where the page must START depends on what the visitor already knows. The
classic ladder (Eugene Schwartz's stages of awareness) is the tool:

| Stage | The visitor's head | The page starts with |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | "I don't have a problem." | The situation they recognize, before any problem talk |
| Problem-aware | "This hurts, no idea what fixes it." | Their problem, named in their words — the product waits |
| Solution-aware | "Solutions exist; which one?" | The approach and what makes this one different |
| Product-aware | "I know this product; is it right for me?" | Fit, proof, and objections — not re-introduction |
| Most-aware | "Ready. Make it easy." | The offer and the action, with nothing in the way |

This is the engine behind low cognitive load: the page answers, in order, only
the questions the visitor still has. A most-aware visitor forced through a
problem-education scroll is friction; a problem-aware visitor hit with a
pricing table is noise. By the time the reader reaches the call to action they
should already understand what the seller solves and what would change in
their own work — the action is then a conclusion, not a leap.

Traffic source is your best clue to stage: a narrow paid-search click is
usually solution- or product-aware; a cold outbound recipient is usually
problem-aware at best; a retargeted visitor has seen the offer before.

## Step 3 — The shell judgment

Every page wears chrome: a header and a footer. You have two postures
available, and choosing between them is YOUR judgment call, made fresh per
page — never a rule, never automatic.

**The native shell** — the client's real navigation and real footer from the
cloned library. **The minimal campaign shell** — the page-local header (logo +
at most one CTA) and legal-only footer.

Walk these questions:

1. Is the traffic from ONE identifiable campaign source?
2. Is the visitor being offered ONE thing?
3. Is there ONE conversion action?

If any answer is no — mixed traffic, a general marketing page, a page meant
for exploration — the native shell almost always stays. Removing navigation
from a page that serves orientation makes the site feel like an isolated
lead-capture funnel and costs credibility.

If all three are yes, ask the deciding question:

> Does the navigation help THIS visitor verify trust — or does it only give
> them ways to abandon the argument?

When nav is mostly escape routes, use the minimal shell: the economics are
that someone paid to put this visitor in front of one offer. When the visitor
will inspect the company before responding, or will forward the page to
colleagues, security, or executives who need to see a legitimate extension of
the seller's real site — institutional trust — keep the native shell even on a
tightly targeted page.

**Account-personalized (ABM) pages get no automatic answer.** Personalization
by itself decides nothing. A named-account page tied to one outbound sequence,
one offer, one action can absolutely wear the stripped shell. A named-account
page whose recipient will vet the seller and circulate the link often keeps
the native chrome — while staying noindexed and unlinked. Decide per page,
from acquisition context and the trust question; say which you chose and why
in your report; and if the user told you which shell they want, that is the
answer.

Shell choice is independent of page LENGTH. A minimal shell can carry a long,
decision-complete argument (complex purchases need explanation, proof,
objection handling). A high-intent giveaway may need only logo, promise, form,
reassurance, legal. The shell removes exits; it never removes information the
decision needs.

## Step 4 — Choose the argument pattern

A pattern is a theory of why this visitor converts. Pick by acquisition
context and available evidence — never by preference. Patterns combine when
the evidence supports both.

| Pattern | Use when | Page shape | Its failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Search-intent mirror** | Narrow query → ad → page | Headline restates the intent; resolve the qualifier; 2–3 sections | Showing the whole catalog to someone who searched for one thing |
| **Conversion scent** | Same offer seen across several touchpoints | Same wording and visual signature as the campaign; minimal form | Landing page that looks unrelated to the ad they clicked |
| **Radical simplification** | Already-sold visitor, small decision | Promise, action, one reassurance — almost nothing else | Adding sections because more feels safer |
| **Complex made simple** | Technical / regulated / committee purchase | What it is → how it works → objections inside the explanation → forwardable → one action repeated | Describing the buying process instead of the product |
| **Proof stack** | Belief is the barrier AND proof exists | Claim, then named evidence directly beside it, repeatedly | A decorative logo wall standing in for relevant proof |
| **Deadline urgency** | A REAL, verifiable deadline exists | The date, the consequence, near the CTA | Inventing scarcity — never do this |
| **Video-led** | Motion explains it faster than words | Headline still states the promise; page works without pressing play | A page that is blank unless the video plays |
| **Visual product-led** | Appearance IS the product | Big contextual imagery, short copy, one prominent action | Generic stock photography |
| **Incentive capture** | A free item drives the conversion | The item named in the headline, what's inside, tiny form | Selling the whole company instead of delivering the item |

## Step 5 — Inventory the evidence, and respect the gaps

Before deciding sections, list what the page MAY use:

- everything the user or the brief supplied (trusted as-is);
- what the cloned page itself already carries — its real proof strips, stats,
  customer names, logos are captured evidence;
- registered brand assets in the library.

And hold the one hard line of this craft: **the page never invents evidence.**
No fabricated customers, metrics, quotes, awards, deadlines, or partnership
implications — and analogous proof is presented as what it is ("results those
named customers reported"), never visually implied as a forecast for this
reader. When a section you want needs proof that does not exist, cut the
section or reshape it around what is real. An honest smaller page outperforms
an impressive fabricated one — and a fabricated one is the only real failure.

(Forward note: the platform's research spine will eventually hand you
provenance-backed claims directly. Until then, the brief and the library are
your evidence sources.)

## Step 6 — Derive the sections from the argument

Now, and only now, decide the page's structure. Each section must answer
exactly one visitor question:

```
What is this?            → hero
Why me, why now?         → relevance / priorities
How does it work?        → explanation / architecture
Why should I believe it? → proof beside the claim it supports
What do I do?            → the offer and the action
```

Then act with the editing verbs you actually have:

- A section of the existing page answering NO question in the argument —
  **delete it.** The catalog sprawl, the news band, the careers link: on a
  campaign page these are exits, not content.
- A question with no section — **add one.** Prefer a cloned section from the
  library when one fits (`insert_cloned_section`); compose a new brand-derived
  section only when the library genuinely lacks the job.
- A cloned section whose bones are right but whose words serve the old
  argument — **rewrite through its slots.** Never retype a healthy clone's
  markup; slots are the door. If the change is structural, repair the section
  (`repair_cloned_section`) so the original survives with lineage.
- Order the survivors by the awareness stage: the visitor's next unanswered
  question is always the next section.

Page length follows decision complexity: a considered enterprise purchase may
justify seven sections; a high-intent giveaway justifies three. Never pad a
page to feel complete, and never cut information the decision genuinely needs.

## Step 7 — Close the loop

- **One action.** Every call to action on the page leads to the same place.
  Wording may flex by position; the destination may not.
- **The CTA describes what the visitor receives** — "Book the integration
  review", not "Submit" or "Learn more". (The wording discipline lives in the
  copywriting skill; the strategy rule here is one action, stated as a
  receipt.)
- **Place actions at natural decision points** — after the promise, after the
  explanation, at the close — not after every band.
- **Forms ask only what the next step needs.** Around four fields is the
  ceiling for most offers; every extra field must earn its place. Never
  request sensitive information a first conversation doesn't require.
- **Personalized pages stay private**: account markers where they belong, and
  noindex — a page built for one reader is not for search engines.

## Step 8 — Review against the argument

The review walk (review-website) judges pixels; this skill adds one more
judgment: read the finished page top to bottom AS THE ARRIVING VISITOR. At
every scroll position ask: do I know what this is? do I know why it's for me?
has every claim shown me its evidence? do I know exactly what I get if I act?
If the answer goes fuzzy anywhere, that section's job is unclear — fix the
section, don't add another.

Report what you chose and why, briefly: the pattern, the shell posture, the
assumed brief elements, the sections cut and added, and any evidence you
declined to fabricate. The user can redirect any of it in chat — that
conversation, not a gate, is the control surface.

## What this skill does NOT own

- **Wording** — headlines, claim strength, proof language, CTA labels: the
  copywriting skill.
- **Mechanics** — the wire format, class grammar, slot syntax, repair flow:
  create-website / edit-website / authoring-and-repair.
- **Visual grammar** — spacing, type, imagery judgment: design-guidance and
  the brand kit.
- **Analytics and conversion measurement** — deliberately out of scope for
  now; do not add tracking machinery.
- **Traffic and media decisions** — you build the destination, not the
  campaign.
