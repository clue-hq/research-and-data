# Conversion Copywriting — the words are the argument

Read this whenever you are writing page copy: authoring a new section,
rewriting a cloned band through its slots, labeling a button, or wording a
form. The campaign-landing-page skill decides WHAT the page argues; this
skill decides HOW every sentence says it.

## Why this skill exists

Page copy fails in predictable, recurring ways:

- The headline sounds ambitious but never says what the thing IS.
- The language is the seller's internal vocabulary, not the reader's.
- The page speaks to everyone and therefore lands with no one.
- Adjectives stand in for evidence ("powerful, flexible, world-class").
- A customer's result quietly mutates into a promise for THIS reader.
- Cleverness demands interpretation the visitor will not spend.
- Two calls to action compete and the reader chooses neither.

The counter-principle has measured true for a century: **specific statements
carry their full weight; platitudes roll off.** "Combine seats, included
usage, credits, and overages on one billing foundation" persuades; "unlock
the future of intelligent growth" describes nothing and convinces no one.

The purpose statement:

> Convert the evidence you were given into the clearest possible language for
> the specific reader — their vocabulary, their order of questions, claims no
> stronger than the evidence, proof beside every claim, and one action stated
> as what the reader receives.

Nothing here is a gate. These are the judgments that make copy good; the user
can override any of them in chat, and material the user supplies is trusted
and used as-is.

## The reader test

Before writing a word, be able to answer:

```
Who is this for?
What is being offered?
Why is it relevant to them, now?
What does it actually do?
What evidence supports each claim?
What should they do next, and what happens after?
```

The copy's whole job is to make those answers effortless, in that order —
the pyramid: what this is → why it matters → how it works → evidence →
detail → action. A reader who stops scrolling at any point should still have
gotten the most important thing above that point.

## Say what it is

The first screen is never a mystery box. For a considered decision, literal
beats clever — a visitor who must interpret the headline pays a tax you set.

Judge every headline on five things:

1. **One-read meaning** — understandable without a second pass.
2. **Reader ownership** — about a decision or outcome THIS reader owns.
3. **Specificity** — names the system, job, or result; no category fog.
4. **Evidence compatibility** — the page can back it (see the claim ladder).
5. **Geometry fit** — it renders correctly where it lands (see Copy through
   slots — on this platform that rule has teeth).

A working headline formula: *outcome or system + reader context + a
meaningful qualifier*. And the subheadline EXPANDS the headline — scope,
components, qualification — it never restates it in different words.

## The reader's vocabulary

Perform this transformation on everything you were given:

```
Seller capability language  →  Reader operational language
"Multidimensional usage-based billing"
→ "Connect seats, model usage, contracts, and overages in one pricing system"
```

Prefer the words the audience actually uses — from the brief's research, the
account's own materials, and the clone's own published copy. Investor pitch
is not buyer pitch: market size, category creation, and vision are OUT;
what changes operationally for the reader is IN. If the brief carries the
reader's own phrases, use them verbatim — recognition is persuasion.

## The claim ladder

Every claim sits at the strongest level its evidence supports — and no
higher:

| Level | Example | Needs |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Product fact | "Supports usage-based billing." | Product truth |
| 2. Capability | "Can meter usage and combine it with subscriptions." | Documented capability |
| 3. Customer result | "Warp: overages reached 20% of its business." | The result, ATTRIBUTED to its owner |
| 4. Reader implication | "This could give you a clearer expansion path." | Conditional language — <em>could</em>, <em>may</em> |
| 5. Reader forecast | "This will increase your revenue 20%." | NEVER, without reader-specific evidence |

Verb discipline carries the ladder: **will** only for operational certainty
(clicking opens the form), **can / supports** for capability, **could / may**
for opportunity, **helps** when the product contributes but isn't the sole
cause. And the transformation-word test: "unlock", "supercharge",
"revolutionize", "future-proof", "best-in-class" must be cashed out into
something checkable — or cut. The specific sentence sounds smaller and
persuades more.

## Proof language

- **A metric stays welded to its owner.** "Hex sent 500M+ usage events in two
  months" — never "process 500M+ events" floated free, and never a customer's
  number rewritten as this reader's future.
- **"Trusted by" means actual use.** Investors and partners are "backed by".
- **Analogous proof is labeled as what it is** — results those named
  customers reported, evidence for the model, not a forecast — and the
  qualification sits BESIDE the claim, in body text, never buried in a
  footer.
- **Relationship honesty on personalized pages**: "prepared for X", "a
  proposal for X" — never "X uses us", "our work with X", or a co-brand lockup
  implying a partnership that doesn't exist.
- **Nothing is invented, ever** — no quotes, logos, numbers, awards, or
  deadlines that were not supplied or captured. An empty proof slot is a
  section decision (cut or reshape), not a writing prompt.

## Calls to action

- The label describes **what the reader receives**: "Book the integration
  review", "Get the benchmark", "Plan the strategy session" — never
  "Submit", "Learn more", "Get started", "Click here".
- **One action, everywhere.** Wording may flex by position ("Plan the
  session" in the header, "Request the session" on the form) but every
  button leads to the same place.
- **Short.** The button is not the value proposition; the sentence above it
  is. Aim for 2–5 words.
- **First person converts.** Where the brand's voice allows it, "Start MY
  free trial" reliably outperforms "Start YOUR free trial" in testing — the
  reader clicks a decision, not an instruction.

## Form and interaction copy

- Field labels are plain nouns; the form headline restates the offer, not
  the company.
- One reassurance line near the button — what this is and is not ("We'll use
  this to prepare the session. No mailing list.").
- Every field earns its place: fewer fields usually completes better, but a
  field the reader WANTS to answer ("what should the session solve?") earns
  its place by improving the conversation both sides get. The test is the
  offer's next step, not a field count.
- Success copy says what happens next; error copy is calm and blames no one.

## Structure and scannability

Visitors scan before they read. Therefore:

- **One question per section** — a section that answers three questions
  answers none well.
- **Parallel construction** in every list: same shape, verb-first
  ("Define the decision / Map the data / Outline the plan").
- **The hierarchy carries the value**: the most important proposition lives
  in the headline, never in small supporting text under an abstract banner.
- Short paragraphs, meaningful headings, metrics up front in proof cards.

## Copy through slots — the rule with teeth on this platform

When you rewrite a cloned band, your words land in **slots inside fixed,
captured geometry**. The type scale, line lengths, and layout are the
original site's, byte-exact — which turns "copy must fit the composition"
from advice into physics:

- **Match the original's approximate length and line count.** A slot that
  held six words wants roughly six words back, not twenty. A headline slot
  wraps to the same one or two lines it wrapped to on the source site.
- **The clone's original copy is evidence.** It is the seller's own
  published, market-tested language. A rewrite should read like the same
  author writing a more specific sentence — same cadence, same vocabulary
  register — never like a stranger pasted text into their site.
- **When the argument needs more room than the slot's geometry bears, that
  is an edit-escalation decision, not an overflow**: repair the section or
  compose a replacement band. Never cram, never let a rewrite break the
  band's rendering.
- **Personalization markers count at rendered size.** `{{account.name}}`
  occupies the length of a real company name, not the marker's own text —
  write the sentence for the rendered result.
- **How the words actually land**: a slot rebind is `set_node_props` on the
  fragment node with the full payload (edit-website's clone-editing ladder,
  rung 1) — or the `slot_values_json` argument of the same call that pins
  the band (`assemble_cloned_sections` on an empty page,
  `insert_cloned_section` after). Write the copy first, then bind it in the
  pinning act itself when you can — one act, no intermediate render of the
  band wearing the wrong words.

## Tone — where the voice comes from

In order of authority:

1. **The brand voice / style guide, when the kit carries one.** (Brand-kit
   ingestion will attach a voice-guide document for some sellers; when it
   exists, it is the authoritative tone source — follow it over everything
   below.)
2. **The brand card and the anchor** — the brand context you receive every
   turn, and the 2–3 creative-director words distilled from it. A
   calm-authoritative infrastructure brand and a playful consumer brand are
   different disciplines of restraint; write inside the one you were given.
3. **The clone's own copy** — the live reference for cadence, sentence
   length, and vocabulary register.

Whatever the tone: account-specific caution survives it. Even the most
confident brand voice does not manufacture certainty about this reader's
business.

## Positive framing

Lead with the operating state the reader gains, not an accusation about
their present ("keep pricing flexible as usage grows" — not "stop letting
outdated billing hold you back", which assumes facts about the reader and
picks a fight). Real problems are named plainly, framed toward the desired
state.

## What this skill does NOT do

- **Strategy** — audience, offer, one action, argument pattern, shell,
  section list: campaign-landing-page owns those; copy fills them.
- **Research** — it consumes what the brief and the page supply; it never
  investigates and never invents.
- **Visual grammar** — spacing, type, imagery: design-guidance and the brand
  kit.
- **Cleverness** — memorability is welcome only when meaning survives one
  read. When forced to choose, choose clear.
