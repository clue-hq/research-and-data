# Page copy

The words are the argument. They turn what you know into the clearest language for this reader:
their vocabulary, their order of questions, claims no stronger than the evidence, proof beside each
claim, and one action stated as what they receive. Clear beats clever: a reader who has to work out
what a line means will not.

Write every visible sentence yourself, in the brand's voice. Use the facts from the brief and the
research; never lift their sentences onto the page. Which facts you may use is in `evidence`; this
skill says how to write them.

The examples in this skill are made up, to show each idea. Most use one imaginary seller, Northwind,
whose software matches and approves supplier invoices, and one imaginary account, Lakeside Group.
Take inspiration from them; never copy their words, and never use them as facts.

## Short statements, not slogans

- Visitors scan before they read. Use short headings, concise paragraphs, lists, figures and
  parallel cards, and keep sentences simple. Repeated items follow one pattern (for example an
  eyebrow, a headline and one short explanation). Depth comes from more short pieces, not longer
  ones.
- No paragraph runs over 20 words.
- State the claim; don't spell out the reasoning behind it. Write statements that matter to the
  reader, not the logic you used to reach them.
- Be specific at the right level. Too vague: "Improve finance efficiency." Too technical for a hero:
  "Configure approval matrices, three-way matching, coding rules and ERP sync." Right for a hero: "Get
  every supplier invoice matched, approved and ready to pay in one place." The technical detail comes
  later on the page.
- Explain the product: what it does and how its parts fit together. A sequence like "Book a call →
  We learn your needs → We propose a plan" describes a buying process, not the product.
- A line that names no product action, system or result, or would fit any company, is a slogan.
  Make it specific, or cut it.

## Claims

Every claim sits at the strongest level its evidence supports, and no higher:

1. **A product fact**: "Supports three-way matching."
2. **A capability**: "Can send each invoice to the right approver automatically."
3. **A named customer's result**: "Harbor & Co. cut invoice approval from 9 days to 3 in 2025."
4. **What it could mean for this reader**: a "could" statement in plain words, joining the seller's
   documented capability to the reader's sourced situation: "could give Lakeside's finance team a
   clearer view of what is waiting to be paid." It is an inference, never written as a fact.
5. **A forecast for this reader**: never.

- Never, for this reader: a number, a percentage, a money amount or time saved, "will" about their
  outcome, or any certainty about their result. Numbers appear only as named customers' results.
- Choose verbs on purpose: **will** only for what certainly happens ("clicking opens the form");
  **can** for what the product does; **could** for this reader's possible benefit; **supports** for
  how it is built; **helps** when it contributes but is not the sole cause.
- Words like "unlock", "supercharge", "revolutionize", "best-in-class" are either made concrete (what
  exactly happens) or cut.
- A customer's number always carries whose result it is, in the same line: "Harbor & Co. cut invoice
  approval from 9 days to 3." A short condition that goes with a number (a time window, a plan, a
  region, a company size) stays in the same sentence or in the proof's source line: "… in its first
  year." Which numbers may be used at all is in `evidence` ("Numbers").

## Voice and the buyer's words

- **Voice** comes from, in order: a brand voice or messaging document in `seller/` when there is one;
  the seller's own site copy as its sections carry it (cadence, register, sentence length); the
  brief's tone notes.
- **Then tune it to the seller's buyer.** Decide what this buyer trusts. A CFO, for example, may
  trust language that is exact, measured and careful with numbers, with no hype; a team lead who uses
  the product every day may want it plainer and warmer, about the work in front of them. Reason from
  the buyer in front of you; these are examples, not settings.
- **Use the words the buyer already uses**: the account's quoted words, its own site, the ICP and
  persona documents. Turn the seller's internal terms into the buyer's working terms ("end-to-end
  procure-to-pay orchestration" becomes "get supplier invoices approved and paid on time").
  Write a customer pitch, not an investor pitch: market size and vision are out; what changes in the
  buyer's work is in. Explain a product name the first time you use it, and never stack jargon.

## Headlines

A headline passes six tests:
1. It is understood in one read.
2. It is about something the buyer owns: their decision, their work, their result.
3. It is specific: it names the system, the job or the result.
4. The page can back it up.
5. It fits its space: it does not wrap much worse than the original, and leaves no single word alone
   on a line.
6. The line under it expands it (scope, parts, a condition) and never repeats it.

A working shape: the outcome or system, plus the buyer's context, plus a qualifier that means
something. No category slogans, no forced puns, and no repeating the account's name in every
headline. On pages for one account, one person or a few named accounts, the hero describes what the
seller proposes, not a result; elsewhere, a result in a headline needs attributed proof further down.

## Buttons and the offer

- A button names what the buyer receives or what happens, in two to five words: "Request the
  invoice review", "Get the approval analysis", "See your approval flow mapped". Never "Submit", "Learn
  more", "Explore", "Get started" or "Talk to sales": those describe the seller's process, not what the
  buyer gets. The sentence above the button carries the value.
- The wording may vary a little with its place while every button leads to the same offer and
  destination: "Book the review" in the header, "Book the invoice review" in the hero, "Request your
  review" at the close.
- The offer section answers, in short pieces: what will happen, who it is for, what will be covered,
  what the visitor leaves with, and how much they need to give to begin.
- Never promise what nobody established: a response time, how their details are handled, a delivery
  or a follow-up.

## Talking about the prospect

- Lead with the working state the buyer gains. Never scold them about how they work today; name a
  real problem plainly and point it toward the better state.
- Never describe the prospect's own product negatively; say what the seller adds.
- No buying-committee labels ("economic buyer", "champion"). People at the account are never named,
  except the reader of a page made for one person (`page-kinds`).
- **Relationship words.** Never write, about the account, without evidence of the relationship:
  partner, customer, working together, joint solution, "our work with <account>", "<account> uses
  <seller>", "trusted by <account>", "<seller> × <account>". Safe framing: "Prepared for <account>",
  "A proposal for <account>", "<seller> for <account>", "A tailored proposal". "Trusted by" and
  "customer" apply only to real customers. The same lists apply to the header's line.

## Rewriting a clone's words

Keep what still fits; rewrite the words that are generic or speak to the seller's homepage audience.
Write like the same author: the same cadence and register. A rewritten line should not be overly
verbose. When the material needs more room than the slot gives, never cut the material: rebuild the
section or choose another (`building-with-puck`, "Reuse first").

## Off the page

None of this ever appears on the page; it goes to `plan.md` and your report:
- the brief's Notes for Design;
- the research's Watch-outs and Context only sections;
- notes on how to approach the account (who to call, what to offer, where the seller overlaps or
  competes);
- file names, line codes and claim labels;
- your own caveats, strategy, reasons and the limits of the evidence.

A sentence that says what the page is not, what the evidence is not, or what you were told to avoid
is a note that leaked ("These are vendor case studies, not results for you", "Not a generic signup").
Say what is true instead, or cut it. The one exception is the short line near customer proof on a
page for one account or one person (`page-kinds`), which says the results are each named customer's
own: keep it.

## The final check on the words

`page-review` runs this on the visible words. Read the page as its reader would, then fix each line
that fails:
- **Mystery headline**: it sounds ambitious but does not say what the offer is.
- **Buried offer**: the offer appears only below abstract sections.
- **Investor pitch**: it talks about the category instead of value to this reader.
- **Speaking to everyone**: it addresses no one in particular instead of this reader, group or
  audience.
- **More than one primary offer**: two offers or destinations compete.
- **Feature dumping**: capabilities listed without tying them to this reader's decision.
- **Benefit inflation**: a capability written as a guaranteed result.
- **A number for the reader**: a figure, percentage or "will" about this reader's outcome.
- **Metric mutation**: a source number turned into a different claim.
- **Unattributed proof**: a number without whose result it is.
- **False relationship**: wording that suggests the account is a customer or partner.
- **Negative framing**: the prospect's setup or product described as failing.
- **Jargon stacking**: several terms in a row with no plain explanation.
- **Repeating subheadline**: the line under a headline repeats it.
- **Generic button**: "Learn more" instead of what happens.
- **Unestablished promise**: a response time, delivery or follow-up nobody established.
- **Slogans and long paragraphs**: a line that would fit any company; a paragraph over 20 words; a
  sentence that explains its own reasoning.
- **Leaked notes**: anything from "Off the page" (the proof line on a page for one account or one
  person stays).
- **People named**: anyone at the account, other than the reader of a page for one person.
- **Swap test**: a sentence that would fit any other account unchanged (product facts excepted).
  Make it specific or cut it.
