# Decision material

For pages for one account, one person or a few named accounts. A page earns its reader's time when
it gives them something to understand and examine, not a profile of their company or a list of the
seller's features.

## Write it before you choose the section

Pick the strongest priority. Name the reader's practical question, the seller's mechanism that
answers it, and the real product evidence you have. Write the explanation and the material first;
then choose the section that can carry them. Too few slots is never a reason to cut the material:
rebuild the clone or choose another section (`building-with-puck`, "Reuse first"), and never shrink
the type.

## What to show for each question

| The reader's question | Material that answers it |
|---|---|
| How would the work change? | A walkthrough of one proposed task: what starts it, what the product does, who or what takes it next, and what the reader would see. |
| Can it meet our criterion? | Their criterion → the seller's documented mechanism → a real product state or relevant proof → what is still to settle. Write the real entries, never those labels as headings. |
| Is there credible evidence? | A named customer's result, attributed, or a real demonstration of the product. Another customer's result is never this reader's forecast. |
| What should our team look at? | A short worksheet of scenarios and what to check, only as an optional later aid: never the whole page, and never filled in with results. |

## Explain before you ask them to evaluate

First give a worked application: what happens, what the product shows or does, and why it matters
for their priority. A page made of "assess", "review" and questions hands the work back to the
reader. The headline states the useful proposition, not your task of evaluating it.

A proposed scenario is not a description of how they work today. The section's heading carries that
("How it could work at …"), so the page needs no caveat.

## Two checks before you build

- **Swap**: if another account's name would fit without changing the material, find the priority
  that is missing. Never invent specificity to pass.
- **Utility**: can the reader say what the product does, and something they can now judge? Feature
  labels, a company biography and decorative screenshots fail.

## An example (made up; take inspiration from it, never copy its words or use its facts)

Aster wants consistent release signoff across the units it acquired. Keel, the seller, has versioned
checklists, named approvals and a status dashboard, and a real screenshot of its approval view.

Weak: "Keel for Aster. Standardize release workflows with powerful AI. Explore checklists, approvals
and dashboards. Book a demo." It repeats the features and invents AI.

Stronger: a section headed "How signoff could work across Aster's units", in three steps:
1. Prepare a unit's release: the checklist version it uses is named.
2. Follow the approvals: who is named at each signoff, with the real approval view beside this step.
3. Bring the review together: the release's status in one view.

It claims no automatic enforcement and no reporting across units, because the seller documents
neither.

## Where the how lives

The wording: `page-copy`. Screenshots and diagrams: `imagery-and-media`. What may be stated:
`evidence`.
